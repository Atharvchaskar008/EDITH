01   The sky above us

Space is not empty.

About 30,000 tracked objects circle the Earth below 2,000 km. Each one covers 7.5 km every second. When two of them cross paths they can meet at 15 km a second. At that speed a loose bolt destroys a satellite, and every crash leaves thousands of new pieces behind.

02   How the sky filled up

Sixty-nine years, one object to thirty thousand.

  1. 1957

    The first satellite reaches orbit. One object, and all the room in the world.

  2. 1978

    Donald Kessler shows that collisions can feed on themselves: each crash makes debris, and debris makes more crashes.

  3. 2007

    A missile test destroys the weather satellite Fengyun-1C. 1,954 of its pieces are still in our catalogue today.

  4. 2009

    Iridium 33, a working satellite, and Cosmos 2251, a dead one, collide at 11.65 km a second. Nobody moved either of them.

  5. 2019

    The first large batch of Starlink satellites is launched. Our catalogue now holds 11,132 of them.

  6. 2021

    Another missile test, another cloud of fragments crossing the paths of working spacecraft.

  7. Now

    The count changes between one download and the next. Somebody has to keep watching.

04   The problem

30,000 objects make 440 million pairs.

Any one of those pairs could be the next collision. Missing a real danger costs a satellite and litters an orbit for decades. Dodging a danger that was never real burns fuel, and fuel is a satellite's lifetime. No person can watch them all, and nobody can afford to guess.

06   How EDITH works

01
Collect

Step one

Download the orbit of every tracked object from two public catalogues.

Step two

Work out where each one will be, every ten seconds, for the next three days.

Step three

Sort the sky into neighbourhoods and compare only neighbours. 440 million pairs become a few thousand.

Step four

For each close pass, work out the chance of a collision, and the worst that chance could be.

Step five

For a dangerous pass, try up to 744 burns and keep the smallest one that makes it safe.

Step six

Fly the new path against everything else for a day. A fix that causes a new danger is thrown away.

Step seven

Say only what changed since last time, and write one page for each dangerous pass.

Step eight

Do it all again in six hours, with nobody pressing a button.

08   Who should move

Not every warning deserves a burn.

Satellite and debrisPlan a burn.

The debris cannot move, so a burn is the only way out. These go first.

Two operatorsPlan, and tell both.

Either could move. EDITH proposes one and writes the warning both can read.

One fleetList it. Plan nothing.

Their operator steers them with data we cannot see. Ours is not good enough to second-guess it.

Two dead objectsWarn.

Nobody can act. Everybody nearby should know.

12   Why one collision matters

Two objects in. Hundreds out.

A crash does not remove two objects from orbit. It replaces them with a cloud, and every piece keeps flying at the same speed. Click the ring to see it.

13   What it cannot do

The honest part.

  • It cannot see anything smaller than about 10 cm. Nobody tracks those in public.
  • It cannot know what another operator is planning.
  • It cannot be more precise than public data, which is rough for anything that manoeuvres.
  • It does not fly satellites. It advises the people who do.
